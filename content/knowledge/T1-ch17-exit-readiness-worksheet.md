# Exit-Readiness Checklist (expanded)

**Source:** The Founder's Field Manual, Chapter 17 — Exit: M&A, IPO, and the Founder's Second Act
**Agent use:** When a subscriber mentions acquisition interest, an inbound from a corp-dev team, banker outreach, or asks "when should I think about exit?" (Answer: 12–24 months before wanting one — readiness is built, not staged.) The chapter's exit examples are illustrations of how different the playbook can look, not templates.

## The six-dimension score (1 = weak, 5 = strong)
1. **Financials** — clean, ideally audited; revenue recognition defensible; no surprises a QofE analysis would find
2. **Legal** — IP secured, contracts tight, cap table clean (T1-ch13 is the feeder checklist)
3. **Operations** — scalable processes, dashboards that don't lie (T1-ch15, T1-ch16)
4. **Team** — strong leadership bench; key personnel retained; the company runs without the founder
5. **Market** — strong competitive positioning and brand (T3-seven-powers: which power would an acquirer be buying?)
6. **Timing** — favorable market or credible inbound interest

**Total /30.** Next step: take the weakest area and build a 90-day action plan for it.

*Score interpretation:* 24+ → you can run a process from strength; 18–23 → fixable in 2–4 quarters, start now; <18 → an exit conversation today happens at a discount, on the buyer's terms. Team is the most commonly overweighted self-score: "runs without the founder" means the founder could take 30 days off during diligence — because diligence will feel exactly like that while the company must not wobble.

## Deal-dynamics notes for the agent
- **The best exits are bought, not sold.** Inbound interest from a strategic who already knows the company beats a banker-run auction for most sub-$100M outcomes — but one credible alternative bidder (or the credible option of *not selling*) is the only real negotiating leverage.
- **An LOI is the beginning, not the end.** Price erodes between LOI and close through diligence findings; every weak checklist dimension above is a re-trade opportunity for the buyer. Exclusivity periods should be short (30–45 days) and earned.
- **Earnouts are deferred disagreements.** Treat any earnout as money at risk, tied to metrics the founder won't fully control post-close.
- **Retention packages vs. purchase price:** buyers shift value from the cap table to employee retention pools; founders with clean vesting and a strong bench keep more of the headline number.
- The agent should always recommend experienced M&A counsel and, above roughly $20–30M, a banker — and flag that it is not a lawyer or financial advisor.

## And one last question (the chapter's)
If your exit closed tomorrow, what would you do the day after? Not the deal-mechanics answer — the personal one. If there's no answer, that is the work to start now, quietly, while the company is still yours. The agent should not let the founder skip this; post-exit drift is the most under-discussed outcome in the book's final part, and unresolved identity shows up *during* negotiations as either desperation to close or inability to.

## Common failure patterns
1. **Staging instead of building** — cleanup sprints when a buyer appears; diligence dates every document.
2. **Falling in love with the first offer** — no alternative cultivated, no leverage held.
3. **Telling the team too early or too late** — too early destabilizes; too late breaks trust with the people buyers most need retained.
4. **Negotiating alone** — the founder as sole point of contact, exhausted into concessions.

## Output the agent should produce
The six scores with one-line evidence each, total /30 with the interpretation band, the weakest dimension's 90-day plan, and the founder's written answer to the day-after question.
