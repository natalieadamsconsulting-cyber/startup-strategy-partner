# Field Notes: 7 Powers (Hamilton Helmer)

**Type:** Original summary — ideas, not text. Written for The Founder's Field Manual AI Agent.
**Agent use:** When a founder asks about moats, defensibility, "what stops Google from copying this?", or is preparing the defensibility slide of a fundraising deck. Cross-reference: business model worksheet (Ch 7), metrics defensibility discussion (Ch 16).

## Core ideas
Power = a structural condition that lets a company sustain differential returns *despite* competitors' best efforts. Each power needs both a **benefit** (margin, price, or share advantage) and a **barrier** (why competitors can't arbitrage it away). The seven:

1. **Scale economies** — unit cost falls with volume (fixed-cost spread, e.g., Netflix content over subscribers). Barrier: challenger must match scale at ruinous cost.
2. **Network economies** — product value rises with users (marketplaces, social, protocols). Barrier: users won't leave the bigger network. Watch: network effects are often *local* (city-by-city for rideshare), which caps the moat.
3. **Counter-positioning** — a newcomer adopts a superior model the incumbent *can't* copy without damaging its existing business (Vanguard's index funds vs. active managers). The startup's most available power. Barrier: the incumbent's rational unwillingness, not inability.
4. **Switching costs** — embedded data, integrations, trained users, contracts. Barrier: customer's cost to leave exceeds challenger's ability to compensate. Compounds quietly in B2B.
5. **Branding** — habitual trust or affective valence built over long, consistent exposure, supporting price premium (not the same as "having a logo"). Slowest power to build.
6. **Cornered resource** — preferential access to a coveted asset: patents, a unique team, exclusive rights, proprietary data.
7. **Process power** — embedded organizational routines competitors cannot replicate quickly (Toyota's production system). Rarest; takes a decade.

**Timing matters:** counter-positioning and cornered resource are available at origination; network and scale economies get locked in during takeoff (the land-grab phase); branding and process power come with maturity. Strategy = acquiring the right power *for your stage*.

## Where it aligns with the Field Manual
Chapter 7's scalable-business-model test is really a power audit: which of the seven does the model earn as it grows? The agent should push every founder to name their *intended* power and the mechanism that builds it — "we'll move fast" is not a power (execution is table stakes, per Helmer).

## Agent watch-fors
- **First-mover mythology:** being first is not a power unless it converts into network effects, switching costs, or a cornered resource before followers arrive.
- **Feature moats:** "no one else has this feature" survives roughly one funding cycle.
- **Fake network effects:** more users → more content ≠ more value per user. Test the marginal-user benefit honestly.
- For AI startups: models are rarely a moat; proprietary data (cornered resource), workflow embedding (switching costs), and counter-positioned business models usually are.
