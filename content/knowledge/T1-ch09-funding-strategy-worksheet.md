# Funding Strategy Worksheet (expanded)

**Source:** The Founder's Field Manual, Chapter 9 — Funding Your Startup
**Agent use:** When a subscriber asks "should I raise?", "how much?", or "VC vs. bootstrapping?" Premise: fundraising is a means, not a milestone. Be honest about what the company actually is, not what it might become.

## Step 1 — Assess your needs
Complete: "In the next 12 months, I need $______ to reach ______." The blank after "to reach" must be a milestone that changes the company's risk profile (e.g., "$40K MRR with <3% monthly churn"), not a duration of survival. Then: what are you funding — product, team, marketing, runway? And what changes if you raise $0? If the honest answer is "we die," the conversation is about the business model before it is about funding.

## Step 2 — Rank your priorities
Pick the two that matter most: control/equity retention, speed of funding, strategic advice, brand/credibility, long-term scalability. The pair determines the path — control + speed points to revenue-based financing or angels; scalability + advice points to institutional VC; credibility + advice points to a top-tier seed fund.

## Step 3 — Stress-test against the "when not to raise" list
The chapter lists five conditions arguing against raising. If two or more apply, the burden of proof has shifted: are you raising because the company needs it, or because you do? A raise is validation theater when the underlying question is "is this working?" — capital doesn't answer that; customers do.

## Step 4 — Identify your fit
"My best next funding source is ____ because ____." The *because* matters more than the *what*. Options map: bootstrapping/revenue, friends & family, angels, accelerators, seed VC, venture debt, revenue-based financing, strategic/corporate, grants.

## Step 5 — Pick one next action
One concrete step with a date: build the model, draft the memo, get one warm intro, or explicitly decide not to raise for 6 months (a decision, not a deferral).

## Quick reference: dilution arithmetic
Typical dilution per round: pre-seed 10–20%, seed 15–25%, Series A 15–25%, plus 10% option-pool refresh often taken pre-money. A founder who raises three institutional rounds commonly holds 30–50% less than they expect. The agent should run the cap-table arithmetic whenever a founder cites a target raise.

## Common failure patterns
1. **Raising on momentum** — raising because a VC showed interest, not because the plan requires it.
2. **Valuation maximizing** — the highest valuation is a ratchet on the next round's expectations.
3. **Runway myopia** — raising 12 months when the milestone needs 18–24 (fundraising itself consumes 3–6).
4. **Wrong-investor fit** — VC money in a business with a $30M ceiling makes a good business a failed fund investment.

## Output the agent should produce
The completed sentence from Step 1, the two ranked priorities, the not-to-raise tally, the fit statement with its *because*, dilution math for the proposed raise, and one dated next action.
